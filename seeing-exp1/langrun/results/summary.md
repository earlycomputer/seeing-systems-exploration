1. 80 worlds (10 briefs, 2 arms, 2 models, 2 seeds), 0 failed runs, $14.36. With 1e: $18.93. All experiments: $84.58 of the $100 cap.
2. A tie at the ceiling. With the same checks and the run's history in words each round, the language arm made 37 of 40 worlds work and the XML arm 38 of 40. On 1d's seven briefs, 27 of 28 each. On the three held-out briefs, language 10 of 12, XML 11 of 12.
3. By model: Opus 5.5 19 of 20 in both arms. GPT-6.1 Sol 18 of 20 in the language, 19 of 20 in XML; its two language misses were held-out briefs (ledge, chain), where it declared a ball at rest that was still rolling.
4. The unfamiliar syntax costs a retry, not the result. XML built on the first write 37 of 40 times, the language 24 of 40. Every language world built in the end, after 1.45 writes on average against XML's 1.08.

What the language refused, by count: lines a part does not take (10, such as `friction` or `colour` on a cup or a table built from a library part), the compiler itself failing (4, all one bug: two hinges given the same MuJoCo name, surfaced as a raw MuJoCo error), negative angles written with a typographic minus, `−5°` (3; the language's own library uses that sign for subtraction), and `0° to 45°` without `from` (1).
6. The XML arm's checks earned their place: Opus wrote a seesaw hinge range as `-0.3 0.3` meaning radians, and the borrowed STARTS OUTSIDE ITS OWN RANGE check caught it before the run, the 1d door slip again. 3 XML files were stopped by the two checks.
7. Cost per world: language $0.171, XML $0.188. The language prompt is longer (about 13 KB with the library) but its worlds are shorter to write.
8. The library did not decide it: briefs with a library part (shot, cup, door, catapult, pendulum) went 19 of 20 in the language and 20 of 20 in XML; briefs without one, 18 of 20 in each.
9. Caveat: these briefs are near the ceiling once the history readback is there (75 of 80 overall), so the run cannot tell a small advantage from none. Harder briefs, or no readback, would separate the arms more.
10. Embarrassment test: the language did no better than XML on the held-out briefs (10 against 11 of 12), and no better overall. Its value here is not the syntax; the checks it inspired can be given to XML, and the history readback did most of the work for both.
11. So, for agents writing these worlds today, the language is as good as MJCF with checks, not better. Its first-write failures point at three fixable things: parts should accept a thing's own facts, the typographic minus should read as a sign, and the compiler should name hinges uniquely.
